Task 3 · LLM-Panel Reader Test

COGS 160 SP26 · Track 4 · Personas P1 (160-student) and P2 (researcher) · Three agents per journey · Conducted per Scaffold 3.3 of the task-design document.

Method

Each agent was instantiated using the Scaffold 3.3 prompt template from the task-design document, substituting the persona profile from the Task-1 panel configurations and the full text of each journey's six steps. Agents were instructed to walk through the journey, note after each step what they understood and what they wanted but did not get, indicate whether they would continue or close the tab, and at the end state which Chinn-Brewer response they would take and why. Runs were conducted using Claude Sonnet 4 (model: claude-sonnet-4-6). Agent outputs below are lightly condensed for legibility but preserve all substantive notes.

Prompt template used (Scaffold 3.3): "You are [persona], a researcher with the following background and commitments: [brief profile]. You have just opened the Knowledge Atlas and are walking through a journey on the question '[journey question]'. Walk through the journey. After each page, briefly note: (a) what you understood; (b) what you wanted but did not get; (c) whether you would continue or close the tab. At the end, indicate which Chinn-Brewer response you would take and why."

Question: "How strong is the evidence that humans prefer environments with prospect and refuge?" · Persona: P1-eager (160-student, Maya) · Shape: Toulmin · File: journey_mq010_prospect_refuge.html

(b) Wanted: I wanted to know how large the reversal effect was — is d for the reversal similar in magnitude to the positive effect? (c) Continue: yes.

Step 5 — Chinn-Brewer Panel

(a) Understood: All seven responses and their implications. The responses were specific enough to this journey's defeater that they felt useful rather than generic. Chinn-Brewer response taken
Response 5 — Reinterpret the data, theory unchanged

Reasoning: "The urban reversal is real, but it doesn't break the theory — it reveals that the evolutionary cue operates through social threat detection, not just spatial geometry. Prospect is preferred when it signals safety; it is avoided when it signals exposure. What would have made the journey more adequate: I would have benefited from knowing the effect size for the urban-reversal finding — is d in the opposite direction comparable in magnitude to d = 0.43 for the positive effect? Without that number, I cannot assess whether the reversal is as robust as the original finding or smaller and less stable. The journey should quantify the defeater's magnitude, not just its existence.

Agent P1-C — Maya-Sceptic

Agent P1-C: Maya-Sceptic Third-year undergraduate · Has taken a research methods course · Sceptical of evolutionary psychology claims · Looking for reasons to reject the theory
Step 1 — Data

(a) Understood: Meta-analysis with 88 studies. The phrase "ranging from photograph-rating tasks … to VR" raises my concern immediately — these are all artificial stimuli, not real environments. The effect might be a stimulus-evaluation artifact. (b) Wanted: Breakdown of effect size by stimulus type. Is d the same for photograph tasks as for real-environment walkthroughs? If photograph tasks dominate the 88 studies, the generalisability is limited. (c) Continue: yes, but with a flagged concern.

Step 2 — Warrant and Backing

(a) Understood: Evolutionary reasoning. (b) Wanted: An acknowledgment that the evolutionary psychology warrant is contested — specifically, Buller (2005) and critics who argue that the "WEIRD" and photograph-based evidence base cannot confirm an evolutionary mechanism. The journey treats the evolutionary hypothesis as the backing without noting that the backing itself is disputed. (c) Continue: yes, but skepticism increased.

Step 3 — Qualified Claim

(a) Understood: Qualified to WEIRD samples and natural settings. (b) The qualification to WEIRD samples is good but the journey should note that the qualifier is doing a lot of work — excluding WEIRD samples from the claim essentially means the evidence base for the universal evolutionary claim is extremely thin. (c) Continue: yes.

Step 4 — Rebuttal

(a) Understood: Urban reversal. (b) I wanted the journey to acknowledge that Nasar & Fisher (1992) is a counter-claim that requires the same evidential standard as Stamps (2010) — is there a meta-analysis of the urban-reversal literature? If the reversal is based on a few studies, the defeater is weaker than the original claim; if it is meta-analytic, they are comparable in strength. The journey does not clarify this. (c) At this point I would close the tab in a real reading and search for the Stamps/Nasar comparison directly.

Stopped at

Stopped at Step 4 (rebuttal) in a realistic reading. Continued for the purpose of this test but noted that in practice the journey did not resolve the key evidential comparison I needed. Stopped at Step 4

Chinn-Brewer response taken
Response 2 — Reject the data (the original claim, not the defeater)

Reasoning: "The 88-study meta-analysis uses predominantly artificial stimuli. The evolutionary backing is contested. I would reject the core claim as overstated rather than modify my use of the theory — I would simply not cite the theory for architectural design applications, because the evidence base does not support those applications reliably."

What would have made the journey more adequate: The journey would have retained me through Step 4 if it had directly compared the evidential weight of the original claim (Stamps meta-analysis, 88 studies) against the defeater (Nasar reversal — how many studies? Is there a meta-analysis?). A balanced presentation of the two evidence bases would have let me form my own judgment rather than having to search elsewhere. The journey treats the original claim and the defeater asymmetrically in its level of quantification.

Cross-agent finding for Journey 1: Two of three agents completed the journey. All three identified the same structural gap: the journey quantifies the original effect (d ≈ 0.43, 88 studies) but does not quantify the defeater. This asymmetry lets the original claim appear more robust than the challenge without giving the reader the tools to evaluate the comparison. In a revision, the rebuttal step should include an effect-size estimate for the urban-reversal finding and a note on the number of studies in that literature.

Journey 2 · N-Q-005 · Noise→HPA Mechanism Chain

Question: "Broadband noise → HPA → GR downregulation → hippocampal degradation → wayfinding error: which links confirmed?" · Persona: P2-prober (researcher, Nia) · Shape: Toulmin with chain-status table · File: journey_nq005_noise_hpa_chain.html

Agent P2-A — Nia-Surveyor

Agent P2-A: Nia-Surveyor Postdoc, environmental psychology, currently auditing a literature for a systematic review · Needs to assign confidence levels to mechanistic claims
Step 1 — Data (chain-status table)

(a) Understood: The table is well-structured. Four rows, four statuses, clear citations. The badge system (green/amber/red/blue) allows rapid visual scanning. For Step 1, this is good. (b) Wanted: I wanted an indication of the number of human studies for each confirmed link — "multiple studies" for Link 1 is useful but I needed the study count and a range of N to assess what "multiple" means in this field. (c) Continue: yes.

Step 2 — Warrant and Backing (Pearl's hierarchy)

(a) Understood: Pearl's three rungs. I know this framework well. The presentation is clean. (b) Wanted: I would have liked a footnote clarifying whether "intervention-level" evidence requires randomised control or whether quasi-experimental designs with noise manipulation count as Rung 2. In practice, some acoustic studies use within-subject crossover designs that approach Rung-2 status without being randomised. (c) Continue: yes.

Step 3 — Qualified Claim

(a) Understood: Three qualifiers stated clearly. The Toulmin mini-diagram helps. (b) Nothing missing. This step is concise. (c) Continue: yes.

(b) Wanted: A sentence on what a Rung-2 study for Link 2 would look like in practice — what pharmacological agent, what noise-exposure protocol, what sample size. This would help a grant writer translate the critique into an Aim. (c) Continue: yes.

Step 5 — Chinn-Brewer Panel

(a) Understood: All seven responses. Response 4 (hold in abeyance) is clearly the most defensible for a systematic-review context. Response 6 (peripheral change, scope Aim 1 to Link 1) is sensible for a grant context. The panel correctly identifies the strategic logic for each. (b) Wanted: A note distinguishing which responses are appropriate for a systematic review versus a grant application context — the same researcher may need different responses in different writing contexts. (c) Continue: yes.

Completed.

Stopped at

Completed all six steps. Completed

Chinn-Brewer response taken
Response 4 — Hold the data in abeyance

Reasoning: "For my systematic review, I will note Links 2–3 as 'supported by animal models; no Rung-2 human evidence available as of 2026.' I will not drop these links from the review because they represent the field's working hypothesis, but I will assign them a confidence level of 'low' in the review's GRADE-style table and flag the absence of intervention-level evidence explicitly."

Cross-Journey Findings

Finding 1 — Defeater asymmetry in Journey 1

Journey 1 quantifies the original claim (88 studies, d ≈ 0.43) but does not quantify the defeater (no effect size or study count given for the urban-reversal literature). This asymmetry was flagged independently by all three P1 agents. Fix: add a stat-figure block to Step 4 with effect-size data for the Nasar-lineage urban-preference studies.

Finding 2 — Chinn-Brewer panel is persona-neutral in both journeys

Both journeys present the seven responses without editorialising. This is epistemically correct but strategically frustrating for agents who need actionable guidance (P1-A and P2-B). A light editorial note — e.g., "For a student paper, Responses 3 and 5 are most commonly defensible; Response 2 is defensible only if the exclusion criterion is made explicit" — would add practical value without compromising the panel's honesty.

Finding 3 — Alternative mechanisms buried in Response 7

Both journeys mention alternative frameworks only inside Chinn-Brewer Response 7, which many readers will treat as the "give up the theory" response and not engage with. For explorer-persona readers (P2-C), this is where the most interesting content lives. Future journeys should consider linking alternative mechanisms out of the Chinn-Brewer panel into separate pages or at minimum into expandable detail blocks.

Finding 4 — Journey 2 well-matched to P2-prober; under-serves other P2 sub-flavours

The noise→HPA journey is optimised for a P2-prober who wants to audit the chain's evidential status. It serves the P2-surveyor and P2-frontiersman less well. The task-design document notes that each student owns one persona; in a real deployment, the journey would be paired with its specific sub-persona and this mismatch would not arise. In this test it surfaces useful information about the range of needs within a single persona tier.
