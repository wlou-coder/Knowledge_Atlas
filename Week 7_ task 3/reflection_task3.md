## What Moving from Spec to Prototype Revealed

The most consistent lesson from building the two prototype journeys is that the spec conceals the sequencing problem. In Task 2, fitting a question to an answer shape requires specifying what goes in each structural slot — data, warrant, claim, rebuttal — and the spec can be completed coherently at that level of abstraction. But when the spec becomes a page sequence that a reader actually traverses, the order of disclosure suddenly matters in ways that the spec cannot capture. For the P1 journey (M-Q-010, prospect-refuge evidence), the spec listed the urban-reversal defeater as a named counterargument in the rebuttal slot. Implementing that defeater as a web page forced a decision the spec had deferred: should the reader encounter the qualifier first (in the claim) or the defeater first (in the rebuttal)? The Q-29 exemplar's Toulmin sequence — data, warrant, claim, rebuttal — places the qualifier with the claim and the defeater immediately after it, which means the reader has already accepted the claim before meeting its strongest challenge. Whether that sequencing is honest or rhetorical is a design question that does not exist at the spec level.

The P2 journey (N-Q-005, the noise-to-wayfinding mechanism chain) surfaced a different kind of gap. The Task-2 spec described the chain's evidential status in one paragraph — "Links 1 and 4 supported; Links 2–3 inferential." Implementing that summary as a chain-status table with four rows and four evidential badges immediately revealed that the summary had smoothed over real heterogeneity: the meaning of "confirmed" for Link 1 (multiple cross-laboratory observational studies in humans) is not the same as the meaning of "partial" for Link 4 (confirmed in clinical populations at clinical levels of hippocampal damage, not confirmed in sub-clinical noise-stressed cohorts). In the spec, both had been described as "supported." In the prototype, they required different badge colours and different prose qualifications. The prototype made the summary's internal inconsistency visible in a way that the spec had allowed me to avoid.

## Where the Spec Was Ambiguous at Implementation

The most consequential ambiguity in the spec was the treatment of the Chinn-Brewer panel. The Task-3 rubric requires the panel to be "exposed by default" — not hidden behind a toggle — and the Q-29 exemplar implements this by rendering the panel visible on the page with items that expand on click. But "exposed by default" can mean several things. Does it mean all items are visible at page load with their full text? Does it mean the panel itself is visible but items require a click to open? Does "exposed" refer to the panel's position in the page hierarchy or to the level of disclosure of each item's content? I chose to render all seven responses with their body text visible on load (items closed on click rather than opened on click), which is a more aggressive interpretation of "exposed." The LLM reader tests suggested this was correct for the prober persona (P2-C responded to all seven items in sequence) but slightly overwhelming for the pragmatist sub-persona (P1-A found the page load visually dense). The ambiguity in the spec produces a real design trade-off that the spec cannot resolve without an audience.

A second ambiguity concerned the Toulmin diagram format. The answer-shape catalogue presents the Toulmin shape as a full-page SVG with six labelled positions. The Q-29 exemplar uses a smaller CSS-grid layout (the `.toulmin-mini` component) with two boxes and an arrow — a summary of the inference rather than the full six-position diagram. The spec does not specify which format should appear in the journey prototype. I used the mini-diagram in both journeys, partly because the full SVG is expensive to populate correctly and partly because the journey's prose carries more information than any diagram of reasonable size can contain. Whether that choice serves the reader adequately is a question the spec cannot answer; it depends on what the reader needs the diagram to do, which depends on the persona.

## What the LLM-Panel Reader Test Surfaced

The most consistent finding across all six agents was that the journeys quantify their original claims but not their defeaters. Both journeys present the central evidence with effect sizes, study counts, or chain-status ratings. Neither journey quantifies the defeater with the same precision. In Journey 1, the Nasar-urban-reversal finding is described qualitatively but its magnitude (how large is the reversal? how many studies?) is not stated. In Journey 2, the absence of Rung-2 evidence for the interior links is described as a gap but the size of that gap — how far are the acoustic-stress levels in human office studies from the levels that produce hippocampal changes in rodent protocols? — is not estimated. All three P1 agents and all three P2 agents independently flagged this asymmetry. A journey that presents the claim at high precision and the defeater at low precision is doing something epistemically suspect: it is making the original inference appear more reliable than the challenge, which is not intellectually honest. This is the single finding from the reader tests I am most confident generalises beyond these two specific journeys.

The second finding was persona-specificity within the Chinn-Brewer panel. The panel is designed to be epistemically neutral — it presents all seven responses without recommending any. That neutrality is correct in principle, but the reader test revealed that it fails sub-personas who need strategic guidance, not just epistemic completeness. The advocate sub-persona (P2-B) explicitly wanted the panel to tell her which response was defensible in a grant-review context. The pragmatist sub-persona (P1-A) wanted to know whether her specific use-case context qualified for the exclusion-from-domain response. Neither of these needs can be met by a neutral panel; they require the journey to make a recommendation that is conditional on the reader's situation. The journeys cannot make that recommendation without knowing the reader's context — which they do not collect. This is a structural limitation of the single-persona-ownership model that the reader tests made visible.

## What I Would Change with Three More Weeks

The first change would be to add evidential quantification to every defeater panel. The chain-status table in Journey 2 is an attempt at this — it gives status labels rather than just prose descriptions — but it does not go far enough. With more time, I would build each defeater's evidence base as a second small table: the original claim's evidence count and average effect size on one side, the defeater's evidence count and average effect size on the other. This would let the reader assess the relative robustness of claim and challenge without leaving the journey.

The second change would be to build one sub-persona-specific variant of the Chinn-Brewer panel for each journey. The panel in its current form is generic across the full persona tier. A version targeted at P1-eagerness versus P1-pragmatism — where the "what this response commits you to in your paper" notes are written in the voice and situation of the specific sub-persona — would address the strategic-guidance failure that all advocate- and pragmatist-type agents identified.

## What I Remain Uncertain About

The question I cannot answer from this prototype is whether the journey is doing what it is supposed to do: changing how readers engage with the defeater, rather than simply providing information they were not previously exposed to. The LLM-panel reader test is a first-pass check. The agents demonstrated that they could navigate the journey, identify the defeater, and take a Chinn-Brewer response. What it cannot demonstrate is whether the act of traversing the journey — rather than simply reading a well-organised summary — changes the quality of the reader's epistemic work. Kirsh's pedagogical claim for the Atlas is that the journey form makes something possible that prose summaries do not: it forces the reader to encounter the qualifier before they encounter the claim, the warrant before they encounter the data, and the rebuttal before they form a fixed judgment. Whether that forcing function operates on real readers, and whether it produces better epistemic outcomes than a well-organised written summary, is a question that only a human user study with appropriate controls could answer. The prototype is a first-generation artefact. Whether it is a good first-generation artefact is what I remain uncertain about.
